# Valuation & Risk Models

*FRM® Part I, published weight 30%.* VaR and ES, option valuation, bond risk, stress testing, credit and op risk.

## What the outline covers

- Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall; coherent risk measures
- Volatility and correlation estimation
- Economic and regulatory capital
- Stress testing and scenario analysis
- Option valuation; the binomial model and Black–Scholes–Merton
- Fixed-income valuation, duration, convexity and hedging
- Country and sovereign risk; external ratings and rating transition matrices
- Credit risk: expected loss, unexpected loss, credit VaR
- Operational risk: loss distributions, capital approaches, Monte Carlo estimation

### Question 1

A portfolio worth $200m has a daily volatility of 1.2% and a mean daily return of zero. The 1-day 99% VaR is closest to:

> V $200m · σ 1.2% · z₉₉ = 2.326

A. $2.4m  
B. $3.9m  
C. $5.6m  
D. $8.5m  

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**C.** VaR = 2.326 × 0.012 × 200m = $5.58m. Using the 95% z-score of 1.645 gives $3.9m, the planted distractor.

*Reading: Valuation and Risk Models — Value-at-Risk*

</details>

### Question 2

A loan has a probability of default of 3%, loss given default of 45% and exposure at default of $10m. Expected loss is:

> PD 3% · LGD 45% · EAD $10m

A. $135,000  
B. $300,000  
C. $450,000  
D. $1,350,000  

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**A.** EL = PD × LGD × EAD = 0.03 × 0.45 × 10,000,000 = $135,000. Provisions cover this; capital covers the unexpected loss around it.

*Reading: Valuation and Risk Models — Credit Risk*

</details>

### Question 3

Which statement about expected shortfall is correct?

A. ES is never greater than VaR at the same confidence level  
B. ES is sub-additive; VaR need not be  
C. ES ignores the shape of the tail  
D. ES requires an assumption of normality  

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**B.** ES is a coherent risk measure and therefore sub-additive, while VaR can violate sub-additivity. ES also always equals or exceeds VaR at the same confidence.

*Reading: Valuation and Risk Models — Coherent Risk Measures*

</details>

### Question 4

Historical simulation VaR differs from delta-normal VaR chiefly because it:

A. Assumes returns are normally distributed  
B. Requires no distributional assumption  
C. Cannot handle option positions  
D. Always produces a lower VaR estimate  

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**B.** Historical simulation reorders actual past returns, so no distribution is assumed — at the cost of needing a long, representative sample.

*Reading: Valuation and Risk Models — Non-parametric Approaches*

</details>

### Question 5

A delta-neutral options book is long gamma. A large move in the underlying in either direction will produce:

A. A loss  
B. A gain  
C. No profit or loss, since the book is delta neutral  
D. A gain only if the move is upward  

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Show the answer</summary>

**B.** Long gamma means the position's delta moves favourably as the underlying moves, producing gains on large moves in either direction.

*Reading: Valuation and Risk Models — Option Sensitivities*

</details>
